Lemon Law Arbitration in New York, NY

We review repair records and vehicle history for lemon law arbitration.

Call (551) 430-4450 Ask a question

People call us saying, “They fixed it, but the light came back,” “My car has been in the shop again,” or “The dealer keeps telling me they cannot duplicate it.” Those are everyday descriptions of a vehicle problem. For arbitration, we translate those descriptions into a repair history: the reported fault, the repair visit, the technician’s notes, and what happened after the vehicle was returned.

Some callers have a stack of repair orders from separate visits. Others have only one invoice, a text message from a dealer, and a dashboard warning that keeps returning. A problem does not need to sound dramatic on the phone for the paperwork to matter; the language on a repair order, the mileage entry, and the dates the vehicle was in the shop can change how the record reads.

This is usually worth looking at now rather than waiting for another repair visit, because the sequence of complaints, inspections, and shop dates becomes harder to reconstruct when repair orders and correspondence are scattered across months of vehicle use.

Warning light and repeat repairs

Owners often tell us that the check-engine light returns shortly after a repair, that the vehicle shakes or jerks when accelerating, or that it stalls after starting. We also hear that the screen stays black, the backup camera cuts out, the heat or air conditioning stops working, or the vehicle will not start even though the dealer said the issue was repaired. Another common call involves a noise from the engine, brakes, suspension, or transmission that was written up differently at each shop visit.

The words used at the service counter matter. “Rattle,” “hesitation,” “loss of power,” and “warning light” may point to one continuing condition, or they may describe separate systems and separate repairs. Two vehicles can sound alike from a driveway, but the repair orders, technician findings, and dates in and out of the shop can lead to very different arbitration questions.

Arbitration file review

We begin an arbitration file review by listening to the vehicle history and identifying the first complaint, the most recent repair visit, and the fault that remains. During the first conversation, we can usually sort out the broad issue: whether the vehicle was purchased or leased, what component is involved, whether the same symptom returned, and which dealer handled the repairs. The documents are needed to confirm the dates, mileage, written complaint, and work performed.

We gather and read the purchase or lease agreement, repair orders, invoices, warranty materials, and correspondence with the dealer. Each repair order is checked for the date the vehicle entered the shop, the date it was released, the mileage, the owner’s stated concern, the technician diagnosis, and the repair listed on the invoice. We then place those records in order so the vehicle history does not depend on memory alone.

We compare the repair timeline with the warranty record and the available arbitration materials. If an entry is missing, vague, or inconsistent with another document, we identify what should be requested from the dealer or located in an email, text thread, service portal, or invoice folder. We also explain what records would be used to present the vehicle history in an arbitration submission.

Repair attempts, shop days, warranty

The number of repair attempts for the same or closely related fault is an important part of an arbitration review. A single visit for a warning light can read differently from several visits where the repair order repeats the same complaint, even if the dealer used different diagnostic language. We look for the connection between the owner’s description, the component inspected, and the repair performed at each visit.

Days the vehicle was unavailable can also matter, particularly when the in and out dates show extended diagnostic work, parts delays, or repeated returns to the service department. Written records carry more weight than a recollection that the vehicle was at the shop “for a while,” so we examine every repair order and loaner or rental document that helps establish the timeline.

We also check whether the fault was reported while warranty coverage was running, whether the vehicle was bought or leased, and who holds the underlying records. A repair order that documents a warning light or loss of function is different from a complaint raised only after coverage ended without a matching service record. The purchase agreement, lease agreement, dealer correspondence, and warranty booklet help us assess how complete the arbitration file is before any submission is considered.

Repair orders and VIN

Before calling, locate every repair order and invoice from the dealer, including copies sent by email or saved in a service portal. Each document may show the complaint, the mileage, the in and out dates, the technician notes, and the work performed. Have the VIN available from the registration, insurance card, or lower windshield area so the vehicle record can be matched accurately. Keep the purchase or lease agreement nearby, along with warranty correspondence, dealer emails, and text messages about appointments or parts.
